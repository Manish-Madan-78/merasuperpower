Report cards, rankings, entrance exams. Much of school life measures one thing: academic achievement. Marks are easy to count, so they get counted.

But research on happiness keeps reaching the same conclusion: marks are only one part of a good life. One of the longest-running studies, Harvard's Study of Adult Development, has followed people for more than 80 years. Its clearest finding is that warm, close relationships predict happiness and health in later life better than wealth, fame or early success. Other long-term studies show that childhood skills such as self-control and getting along with others matter for adult wellbeing as much as test scores do.

So what else goes into the sum?

## The other ingredients of a happy life

- **Emotional intelligence.** Naming feelings, calming down, and understanding how someone else feels.
- **Social skills.** Making friends, working in a team, and repairing things after an argument.
- **Deep knowledge of something.** A subject or skill a child loves and keeps building on, even when it isn't on the syllabus.
- **The right opportunity at the right time.** A teacher who notices, a club that fits, a door opened just when a child is ready to walk through it.

None of these shows up in a mark sheet, yet each one shapes the adult a child becomes. That leaves parents and teachers with three questions.

## Are we teaching children to be resilient?

Every child will fail at something. What matters is what happens next. Resilience is learned when children are allowed to struggle a little, are helped to try again, and hear that a mistake is information, not a verdict.

Neurodivergent children often meet failure earlier and more often than their classmates. A child with dyslexia may have spent years watching friends read easily. That makes resilience even more important for them, and many of them already have more of it than we notice. Our job is to name it and build on it: "That was hard, and you kept going."

## Are we teaching "ethical" smartness?

Being clever is not the same as being wise. Children learn quickly that there are shortcuts: copying homework, gaming a test, telling a teacher what they want to hear. Ethical smartness means using your abilities honestly, and caring about how your choices affect other people.

It is taught mostly by example. Children watch how the adults around them handle a mistake, an unfair situation or a chance to cut a corner. Talk through real dilemmas at the dinner table. Praise honesty even when the honest answer is inconvenient.

Many neurodivergent children have a strong sense of fairness and a strong dislike of rules that make no sense. Given the right guidance, that becomes a firm moral compass.

## Are we teaching them to share and give back?

Sharing a toy at age three grows into sharing time, skills and kindness later. Children who help others feel capable and connected, and both feelings protect their wellbeing.

Giving back is especially powerful for a child who is used to being the one who needs help. Let a child who loves maths tutor a younger cousin. Let a child who knows everything about trains give a talk to the class. Being useful to someone else changes how a child sees themselves.

## Why this matters even more for neurodivergent children

A school system built only around marks can make a neurodivergent child feel like they are always behind. Yet these are often the children with the deepest interests, the most original thinking and the most hard-won resilience. When we value emotional skills, honesty, kindness and expertise as well as grades, we give them many more ways to succeed, and many more reasons to believe in themselves.

## Small things to try this week

- Ask "What was hard today, and what did you do about it?" instead of "What marks did you get?"
- Share a story about a time you failed and what you learned.
- Spot one moment of honesty or kindness and praise it out loud.
- Find one small way for your child to help someone else using something they are good at.
- Make time for their favourite subject, even if it isn't on any exam.

Marks open some doors. Character, kindness and confidence decide what a child does once they walk through them.
