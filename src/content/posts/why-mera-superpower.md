Many years ago I taught young children at Bal Bharati Public School in Delhi. Every class seems to have a child or two who don't fit the pattern. The one who can tell you a story full of detail but can't get it down on paper. The one who knows every answer out loud and loses them all on the test. The one who simply can't sit still, and spends more time being told off than being taught.

Back then we called them "weak", "careless" or "naughty". I didn't have better words, and I didn't have the training to see what was really going on. But I noticed one thing again and again: **the child who struggles in class is rarely the child who isn't trying.** Most of them were trying harder than anyone else in the room.

## What I know now

Today we have better words. Dyslexia, ADHD, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, autism. These aren't labels for children who can't learn. They describe brains that take in, sort and show information differently.

A different brain often comes with real strengths. A child who finds reading hard may be brilliant at seeing how things fit together. A child who can't sit still may have more energy and curiosity than the rest of the class put together. A child who goes quiet in a noisy room may notice details everyone else misses.

The trouble is that most classrooms are built for one way of learning. When a child doesn't fit that way, the message they hear, year after year, is "you're not good enough". That message does more damage than the learning difference ever could.

## Why I'm coming back

After teaching, I did an MBA and spent many years in sales and marketing, at Cafe Coffee Day and Times Internet. It was a long way from the classroom, but it taught me useful things: how to really listen, how to explain something simply, and how to build something from nothing.

I've wanted to come back to children for a long time. Now I'm doing it, and I'm starting with the ones I couldn't help enough the first time.

## So why "Mera Superpower"?

Because I want every child I work with to be able to say it: *this is my superpower.* Not "this is my problem" or "this is what's wrong with me".

That doesn't mean pretending the hard parts aren't hard. Reading may always take more effort. Focus may always need a plan. But a child who knows what they're good at can face the hard parts with confidence instead of shame. That's what I want to help build.

## Three things you can try this week

If you're a parent or teacher wondering about a child in your life, here are three small things to start with.

1. **Notice what they're good at, and say it out loud.** Not "good boy" or "well done", but something specific: "You remembered every step of that recipe." Children who struggle hear a lot about what they get wrong. Make sure they also hear what they get right.
2. **Look at *how* they get stuck, not just *where*.** Do they understand the question when you read it aloud? Do they know the answer but can't write it down? Do they start well and then drift? These patterns tell you far more than marks on a test.
3. **Change one small thing and watch.** Shorter study sessions with a movement break. Reading together instead of alone. Answering out loud before writing. Small changes often show you what a child needs.

None of these replace a proper assessment if you're worried. But they will help you see your child more clearly, and they'll help your child feel seen.
